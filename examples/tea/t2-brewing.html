The four dials

Every cup of tea is the same four decisions:

  • Leaf — how much tea per how much water (the ratio)
  • Water — what is in it (covered in Module 1)
  • Temperature — how hot the water is when it meets the leaf
  • Time — how long they stay together

Change one, taste, change again. That is the whole method.

strength and bitterness are not the same thing. Strength comes from ratio. Bitterness comes from over-extraction — too hot, too long. A strong cup can be smooth; a weak cup can be bitter.

Ratio: start here

A sane starting point is one teaspoon — about 2 to 3 grams — of loose leaf per 200 ml cup. One tea bag is portioned for roughly the same.

Big leaves (whole-leaf oolong, some greens) take up more spoon for the same weight, so lean towards a heaped spoon.

Want it stronger? Add leaf, not time. More leaf gives you more of everything in balance. More time past the sweet spot gives you mostly tannin — the drying, puckering part.

Temperature: match it to the type

Tea Water Why
Green, white 70–80 °C Boiling water scorches them — bitter, flat, “cooked”
Oolong 85–95 °C Enough heat to open rolled leaves, not enough to stew
Black, pu-erh 95–100 °C Robust leaves; a full boil pulls out the malt and body

no thermometer? For green tea, boil the kettle, then wait two to three minutes, or add a splash of cold water. “Just off the boil” is a real instruction, not a fudge.

Time: the sweet spot and the cliff

Black tea — 3 to 4 minutes. By 5, tannins dominate. By 8, it is stewed: bitter, dark, and it will taste that way even with milk.

Green tea — 1 to 3 minutes, and it is far less forgiving. A minute long can be the difference between sweet and harsh.

Oolong — 2 to 3 minutes for the first steep, and good oolong will happily give you three or four more steeps, each a little different.

Whatever the tea: take the leaf out. Tea left sitting in the cup keeps extracting and only gets more astringent.

When it goes wrong

Bitter, drying, harsh → over-extracted. Water too hot for the type, or steeped too long, or leaf left in. Shorten the steep first.

Weak, watery, thin → under-extracted. Not enough leaf, water too soft, or (for black tea) water not hot enough. Add leaf first.

Stewed, flat, “tastes brown” → the pot sat too long, or the water was re-boiled, or both. Nothing rescues it. Start again.

Cloudy when it cools → “tea cream”, a harmless haze of tannins and caffeine binding as the cup drops in temperature. Common in strong black tea and iced tea. A splash of boiling water clears it.

The milk question

The etiquette story. Milk-in-first (“MIF”) protected cheap cups from cracking when hot tea hit them. Fine bone china could take the heat, so milk-in-after (“MIA”) quietly became the “posh” way. George Orwell wrote a whole essay taking the MIA side.

The chemistry story. When you pour tea onto cold milk, the milk warms gradually and its proteins are less likely to denature and clump — arguably a smoother result. Pour milk into hot tea and you briefly scald small amounts of it. Whether anyone can taste the difference is where the argument lives.

if you are serving several people from one pot, milk-in-first makes it very hard to judge strength. Pour the tea, then let people add their own.
