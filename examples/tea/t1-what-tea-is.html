What "tea" actually means

Green, black, oolong, white, yellow, pu-erh — every one of them comes from a single plant, Camellia sinensis. A tea garden in Japan and a tea garden in Assam are growing the same species.

Everything else on the tea shelf — peppermint, chamomile, rooibos, ginger — is a herbal infusion. Perfectly nice. Not tea.

the six tea types are not six plants. They are six ways of handling the same leaf, and the biggest decision is how far to let it oxidise.

Oxidation, the master variable

Tear or bruise a tea leaf and its enzymes start reacting with oxygen. The leaf darkens and the flavour deepens — the same browning you see in a cut apple. The tea maker’s craft is deciding when to stop it, usually by heating the leaf.

Type Oxidation Liquor Character
Green Stopped early Pale gold to jade Fresh, grassy, marine
White Barely handled Very pale Soft, hay, honey
Oolong Partial, 10–80% Amber Floral through to roasted
Black Taken as far as it goes Deep copper to red Malty, brisk, full

what the West calls “black tea” is red tea (红茶) in China, named for the colour of the liquor. Confusingly, Western “red tea” usually means rooibos, which is a South African shrub and not tea at all.

Water is 98% of the cup

Too hard — water full of calcium and magnesium mutes delicate flavours and leaves an oily-looking film (that “tea scum”) on the surface.

Too soft — distilled or heavily softened water has nothing for the tea compounds to bind to, so the cup tastes thin and hollow.

The sweet spot is moderately soft, fresh, well-aerated water. Filtered tap water is usually all you need.

Fill the kettle fresh each time and heat it once. Re-boiled water has lost the dissolved oxygen that makes aroma leap out of the cup.

A very short history

Tea was a Chinese medicine, then a Chinese drink, for centuries before it went anywhere. It reached Japan with Buddhist monks, Europe with Dutch and Portuguese traders in the 1600s, and Britain via a royal wedding — Catherine of Braganza brought the habit to court in 1662.

The British then spent two centuries trying not to pay China for it: smuggling silver, pushing opium, and finally stealing tea plants and expertise to start plantations in India. Assam and Darjeeling are that story’s inheritance.

“orange pekoe” is not a flavour or a type. It is a leaf-grade: a whole-leaf black tea with no orange in it whatsoever.

Reading a tea label

Origin tells you the growing conditions — altitude, climate, soil. Darjeeling sits high and cool; Assam is low, hot and wet.

Flush (for some regions) is the harvest round. First flush is the year’s youngest, brightest leaves; later flushes are deeper and stronger.

Grade describes the leaf, not the quality: whole leaf, broken, fannings, or dust. Tea bags are usually fannings or dust — more surface area, faster and stronger brew, less nuance.

“Blend” means leaves from several gardens combined for a consistent taste year to year. Your everyday builder’s brew is a blend by design.
